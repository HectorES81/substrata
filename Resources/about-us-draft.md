## The Problem with Myers-Briggs (and Tests Like It)

Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) is the most widely used personality test in the world. It is also one of the most criticized by personality researchers.

- MBTI was derived from Jungian theory, not from empirical data about how people actually behave.
- It treats personality as 16 discrete "types" when research consistently shows personality traits exist on continuous spectrums.
- Test-retest reliability is poor: approximately 50% of people score into a different type when retested just five weeks later.
- It was designed for self-understanding and career counseling — not for predicting relationship compatibility.

## The Gold Standard: The Big Five (OCEAN)

The scientific consensus in personality psychology centers on the **Big Five** model, also called the Five-Factor Model (FFM). Unlike MBTI, it was derived from empirical factor analysis — researchers analyzed thousands of personality-descriptive words and found that human personality clusters into five robust, cross-culturally replicated dimensions:

- **Openness to Experience** — intellectual curiosity, creativity, aesthetic sensitivity, openness to new ideas
- **Conscientiousness** — organization, dependability, self-discipline, goal-directedness
- **Extraversion** — sociability, assertiveness, positive emotionality, energy in social situations
- **Agreeableness** — cooperativeness, trust, empathy, prosocial orientation
- **Neuroticism** — emotional instability, anxiety, moodiness, sensitivity to negative stimuli

The Big Five has been validated in 56+ countries and across cultures. It is the dominant framework in academic personality research.

### What the Big Five Predicts for Relationships

A 2024 longitudinal study using nine years of data found that **low Neuroticism and high Conscientiousness are the most reliable predictors of long-term relationship satisfaction** — in each individual partner, not necessarily between partners. Personality similarity between partners matters less than previously assumed; what matters more is the absolute level of emotional stability each person brings.

## Attachment Theory: How We Bond

Developed by John Bowlby and expanded by Mary Ainsworth, and later adapted for adult romantic relationships by Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver, **Attachment Theory** describes the patterns of bonding formed in early childhood that shape how we seek — and avoid — closeness as adults.

Four adult attachment styles have been identified:

- **Secure** — comfortable with intimacy and with independence; responds to conflict with openness
- **Anxious / Preoccupied** — craves closeness, fears abandonment, hyperactivates distress signals
- **Avoidant / Dismissing** — suppresses attachment needs, equates intimacy with loss of autonomy
- **Fearful-Avoidant (Disorganized)** — simultaneously desires and fears closeness; often associated with trauma history

Research consistently shows **Secure + Secure** is the strongest pairing for long-term satisfaction. The most common dysfunctional pairing — Anxious + Avoidant — creates a painful cycle of pursuit and withdrawal that is very difficult to break.

The standard research instrument is the **ECR-R (Experiences in Close Relationships — Revised)**, a 36-item validated questionnaire measuring anxiety and avoidance in attachment.

## Gottman's Research: What Actually Destroys Relationships

John Gottman and his colleagues at the Gottman Institute have conducted the most rigorous longitudinal behavioral research on relationship success and failure. By observing couples discuss a conflict for as little as 15 minutes, Gottman can predict divorce with approximately 90% accuracy.

His most important findings:

- **The Four Horsemen** — four communication patterns that predict relationship failure:
  - *Criticism*: attacking a partner's character rather than addressing a specific behavior
  - *Contempt*: treating a partner with superiority or disgust (the single strongest predictor of divorce)
  - *Defensiveness*: responding to complaints by playing the victim or counter-attacking
  - *Stonewalling*: emotional shutdown and withdrawal from the conversation

- **The 5:1 ratio** — thriving couples have approximately five positive interactions for every negative one during conflict.

- **Emotional bids** — small moments where one partner reaches toward the other for connection. Whether the other partner "turns toward" or "turns away" is a powerful predictor of relationship health.

- **Friendship quality** — Gottman's research shows that the happiest long-term couples describe each other as their best friend. Friendship quality predicts satisfaction more reliably than sexual chemistry over 10+ years.

## Values Alignment: More Predictive Than Personality

Psychologist Shalom Schwartz developed the **Basic Human Values** model — ten universal values organized in a circular motivational structure, validated across 80+ countries:

Power, Achievement, Hedonism, Stimulation, Self-Direction, Universalism, Benevolence, Tradition, Conformity, Security

These organize into two major axes:
- **Self-Enhancement vs. Self-Transcendence** (personal gain vs. concern for others)
- **Openness to Change vs. Conservation** (novelty vs. stability)

Research consistently shows **values alignment predicts long-term relationship satisfaction more reliably than personality similarity**. Where two people stand on children, religion, money, geography, and political engagement are among the highest-stakes compatibility dimensions — and among the most neglected by major dating apps.

## How We Try to Reduce Self-Report Bias

The hardest problem in personality assessment is that people present an idealized version of themselves — who they aspire to be, not who they are. We use several validated techniques to counter this:

- **Forced-choice format**: We ask you to choose between two equally reasonable options rather than rate yourself on a scale. There is no "good" answer — only a true one.
- **Behavioral anchoring**: We ask about specific past behaviors ("In the last month, how often did you...") rather than abstract traits ("Are you a good communicator?").
- **Situational vignettes**: We present realistic scenarios and ask what you would do — probing actual behavioral tendencies rather than self-concept.
- **Reverse-coded items**: Some questions are designed so that agreeing indicates the opposite of what you might expect. This catches response patterns that suggest someone is just agreeing with everything.
- **Partner-perspective framing**: We ask how people close to you would describe you — creating a social reference that tends to produce more accurate self-assessments.
- **Three parallel test versions**: The same underlying dimensions are measured through three different sets of questions. You are randomly assigned a version. This means you cannot know exactly which test you took, making it harder to optimize your answers. It also means our research data triangulates each trait from multiple angles.
